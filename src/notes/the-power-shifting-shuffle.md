The research maps power dynamics and practices inside human rights philanthropy through twenty-five interviews with philanthropic staff, leadership and advisors across seventeen organisations. Its guiding question is whether shifting the power means shifting the business model.

It distils fifteen dynamics, dilemmas and processes. Here are seven of them.

- Practices have run ahead of operations. Every organisation studied had adopted power-shifting practices at some level, clustered in four areas: trust-based philanthropy, flexible funding, localisation, and diversifying leadership. How internal planning, operations and monitoring adjusted to support them varied widely.
- Programme and grantee-facing teams are seen as the frontliners, but finance, HR and communications are central to building, or slowing, the operational infrastructure that makes commitments real.
- New practices collide with old ones. A common example is publicising highly flexible reporting for existing grantees while retaining invite-only processes for new ones.
- Two-thirds of interviewees described staff, leadership, donors and boards as holding vastly different understandings of power shifting within the same organisation.
- Funder–grantee power is discussed openly; interpersonal power inside organisations, such as race, gender, nationality and class in exchanges between leadership and staff, much less so.
- Localisation carries two traps: reducing it to nationality in ways that privilege elites, and channelling funds through intermediaries in ways that outsource power rather than shift it.
- Infrastructure gets built in moments of crisis, when the speed of decision-making, the disruption of norms and the openness to innovation are highest.
