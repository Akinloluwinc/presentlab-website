A contingency plan on file does not show that it works with the staff on duty, at any hour, when a traveller falls ill. This diagnostic helps port health teams and their aviation partners check the evidence for each step in one day, see which functions are in place and which are not, and decide what to fix first.

Built from Present Lab’s field work on airport preparedness and capacity building across five international airports in 2026, the method is published as an open pilot resource. It contains no results, data or site information from any assessment.

## What it checks

Eighteen of the 31 items test the five steps that must work on a live shift: **detect** (recognise an ill traveller), **assess** (interview and examine), **isolate** (separate safely), **refer** (transfer to care) and **notify** (inform the health authority). Thirteen further items check supporting functions across coordination, contingency planning, national surveillance feedback, point-of-use procedures, traveller information, light sanitation and vector checks, and the timeline of the last real event or drill.

Seven items are flagged as **critical functions** — steps without which an ill traveller cannot be safely managed or staff cannot be protected:

- **Trained staff across operating hours** (item 3.1)
- **Initial assessment of an ill traveller** (item 3.4)
- **Screening equipment and in-date PPE** (item 4.1)
- **Assessment and isolation space** (item 4.2)
- **Referral transport** (item 4.5)
- **Designated receiving facility** (item 4.6)
- **Immediate notification pathway** (item 5.3)

## How the day works

The diagnostic is designed for designated international airports and takes one to two weeks of light preparation followed by **one day on site** (a day and a half at large or multi-terminal airports).

Two trained assessors work together throughout — attending every interview, inspection and demonstration as a pair, scoring each module independently before reconciling differences at the end of the day. Every score (0, 1 or 2) must rest on recorded evidence. A score of 2 requires two agreeing evidence methods, including observation or a live test for on-site items and a dated document for record items. What staff on duty cannot open, locate or perform on the day counts as evidence of readiness, not missing data.

The day closes with a provisional readout to the competent authority, critical-function flags first. Documents that already existed on the visit day may be submitted within five working days before the scorecard is finalised.

## What you get

To prevent composite averages from hiding operational failures, the method keeps **three results separate, alongside a floor**:

- **Capability** — points awarded as a share of points possible, reported only where coverage reaches at least 80%.
- **Evidence confidence** — the share of scored items backed by high, moderate or low evidence, flagging any result where weak evidence reaches 25% or more.
- **Coverage and floor** — how much of the review was completed, paired with the lowest score the recorded evidence allows if unscored items were zero.

Critical-function flags sit above the percentage and remain visible whatever the overall band (Red, Amber or Green). A module readout looks like this (fictional example from section 6):

> Module 4 — critical – not verified (4.5); critical – partial (4.2). Capability 60%, Amber. Floor 50%. Coverage 83% (5 of 6). Evidence: 3 high, 2 moderate.

Every priority gap becomes an **owned action** (labelled P1, P2, P3 or Verify) separating immediate containment from longer-term system fixes, each with an accountable role, a target date and an operational closure test.

## How it fits existing standards

The diagnostic is a voluntary operational screen that sits between full, infrequent evaluations. It is written against **IHR (2005)** Articles 19–23 and Annex 1 — noting the **2024 IHR amendments** that entered into force in September 2025 for most States Parties (and September 2026 for others) — and maps to the WHO 2009 Points of Entry assessment tool, SPAR (C11.1 and C11.2), the Joint External Evaluation and WHO Benchmark 17.

On the aviation side, it aligns with **ICAO Annex 9** (Chapter 8), **ICAO Annex 14** (Volume I, section 9.1 and Doc 9137 Part 7) and **CAPSCA**. Where a CAPSCA assistance visit has already taken place, assessors use its report as evidence rather than repeating interviews. For timeliness and improvement, item 6.4 and the follow-on sprint draw on WHO’s **Early Action Review** guidance and the **7-1-7** target.

## From diagnostic to sprint and exercise

A review only matters if gaps close. The methodology note sets out three connected pathways: a **rapid diagnostic** to establish current shift readiness, a **four- to six-week preparedness sprint** focused on three to five priority gaps, and **verification checks** at P1 target dates, sprint end, and 90 and 180 days.

Where a team wants to test revised handoffs after a sprint, [SIMEX](/notes/simex/) — Present Lab’s WHO-standard simulation exercise platform — includes a built-in airport point-of-entry scenario for running and evaluating a tabletop or functional drill.

## What it is not

The diagnostic is not certification, IHR designation, a finding of compliance, an aviation audit, an external evaluation or a ranking of airports, and it does not replace the WHO assessment tool for designated points of entry. It assesses systems, not individuals. The competent authority leads or requests the visit, sees the results first and decides whether and how they are shared.

You can run the diagnostic independently without outside support. To run a visit, you need the methodology note and the **Assessor Field Pack (v1.0)** — an editable XLSX workbook, printable PDF and DOCX field forms, full item anchors and a set of fictional calibration cases. The field pack is available on request via [info@presentlab.org](mailto:info@presentlab.org?subject=Assessor%20field%20pack%20%E2%80%94%20Airport%20diagnostic%20v1.0).

We are inviting port health authorities, airport operators, civil aviation authorities and technical partners to test version 1.0 and share what worked, what did not, and any scoring rules that need tightening for version 1.1.
